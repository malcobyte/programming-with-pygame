# How to Run a Playtest

A playtest is watching someone else play your game — a real person
who hasn't seen it before. This is different from the self-testing you
did in Weeks 14–15: you already know how your game works, so you
can't experience it the way a new player does. Someone else can.

## Before they play

- Don't explain the controls if you can avoid it. If they can't figure
  out how to move, *that's data* — it tells you something your design
  doc doesn't currently handle.
- Say something neutral like "try it out, I'm just going to watch" —
  not "this part's really fun, wait until you see X." Don't set
  expectations; you want their honest reaction, not a reaction shaped
  by what you told them to expect.

## While they play

Stay quiet. Don't help, don't narrate, don't explain. Watch for:

- **Where they hesitate.** A pause usually means something wasn't
  clear — not that the player is slow.
- **Where they smile, lean in, or say something unprompted.** That's
  a moment worth protecting or building on.
- **Where they get frustrated or confused**, and whether they figure
  it out on their own or get stuck.
- **What they do that you didn't expect.** Players almost always find
  something you didn't plan for.

Write these down as they happen, in `PLAYTEST_NOTES_TEMPLATE.md` —
not from memory afterward.

## After they play — asking questions

TALK: "Did you like it?" and "Isn't this fun?" are **leading
questions** — they invite a polite "yeah!" that tells you nothing.
Ask open-ended questions instead:

- "What were you trying to do just then?" (about a specific moment you
  noticed)
- "What did you expect to happen when you did X?"
- "What's one thing you'd change?"
- "What was confusing, if anything?"

Ask at least two of these, and let the answers be as long as the
player wants — don't fill silence for them.

## After the playtest

Pick **one specific thing** you learned and make a real change to
`project/main.py` because of it. Not everything needs to change — one
genuine, traceable fix is the goal.
